Name it · the three ideas today runs on

Discourse analysis

Reading a set of real texts closely for how their language makes meaning and moves power. The premise: wording is never neutral. It frames who matters, who's to blame, and what feels normal.

Example: reading a year of one city paper's headlines about homelessness to see whether unhoused people are written as neighbors in crisis or as a threat to be cleared.

Corpus

The set of texts you gather and study. One kind of text throughout, and enough of them (aim for 15 to 20) that a pattern can surface instead of a one-off.

Example: all 22 eviction notices filed in one county in 2024. A corpus is one kind of text, gathered on purpose. Three tweets, a documentary, and your cousin's rant is not a corpus.

Qualitative vs. quantitative

Quantitative counts (how many, how often). Qualitative reads for meaning (how, and for whom). Discourse analysis is qualitative.

Example: quantitative says the word "illegal" appears in 68% of the articles. Qualitative says what calling a human being "illegal" actually does, and whose interest that serves.
See it · the three ideas in real texts

One event. One stack of headlines.

Four headlines · a city clearing a homeless camp
“City Clears Homeless Encampment Before Festival Weekend”
“Business Group Demands the City Act on Downtown Vagrancy”
“Outreach Teams Scramble to House Families Before the Sweep”
“‘Where Are We Supposed to Go?’ Camp Residents Ask as Bulldozers Arrive”
That's a corpus: four texts of the same kind about one event. Gather 15 to 20 and you can study a pattern instead of a hunch. That's discourse analysis: "encampment / vagrancy / sweep" build a threat to be removed; "families / residents / where are we supposed to go" build people with nowhere to land. Same closure, opposite picture. That's qualitative vs. quantitative: quantitative counts "sweep" or "clear" in three of the four; qualitative reads what "vagrancy" does to a person that "family" quietly undoes.

Build the right stack

What makes a corpus worth analyzing?

Not a corpus

Three news articles, a TikTok, a podcast episode, and a friend's rant about rent.

Mixed kinds, too few. You can't tell a pattern from a coincidence, and there's nothing steady to compare.
A corpus

Every op-ed one state's largest paper ran on immigration in 2024. Or 40 rental listings from one city in one month.

One kind of text, gathered on purpose, enough of them that repetition becomes visible.
The rule: one kind of text, enough of them, chosen to answer a single question.
Read like an analyst

Two events. Two headlines each. What changed, and who does each one protect?

Event 1 · Headline A
"Officer-Involved Shooting Leaves Teen Dead"
Event 1 · Headline B
"Police Officer Shoots and Kills Unarmed 17-Year-Old"
Event 2 · Headline A
"Migrant Caravan Surges Toward the Border"
Event 2 · Headline B
"Central American Families Walk North to Request Asylum"
How to parse it
Take Event 1 first. Same facts, opposite politics, move by move. Word choice. "Officer-involved shooting" is a phrase where no one does anything, a shooting simply "involved" an officer. B names the actor: the officer shoots. Voice and agency. A is passive and cause-free ("leaves dead"). B is active, subject-verb-object. Grammar decides who gets blamed, and A arranges the sentence so nobody does. Present and absent. B says "unarmed" and gives the age. A leaves both out. What a text omits is a choice, not an accident. Whose interest. A shields the institution; B centers the person killed. That is what "language moves power" means. Now Event 2, together: run the same four moves on this pair. What is each headline doing, and who does each one protect?
The moves you make on every text

Five things to look for, again and again

Recurring words. Which words repeat across the corpus?"riot" vs. "protest" vs. "uprising" for the exact same afternoon.
Framing. What picture does the wording paint, what feeling does it reach for?"taxpayer money" (you're being robbed) vs. "public investment" (we're building something).
Present and absent. What do the texts always say, and never say?welfare coverage that names "fraud" constantly and "poverty wages" almost never.
The appeals. Emotion, fear, nature, authority, family, tradition."protect our children" bolted onto a policy that has nothing to do with children.
Whose interest, and who's in the conversation. Who benefits if you believe this, and who gets spoken for but never quoted?a story on a neighborhood's "revitalization" that quotes developers and the mayor, never the tenants being priced out.
Name the sneakiest
A company's statement after a scandal
"We hear our community, and we are committed to doing the work. Mistakes were made, and no one is more disappointed than we are. We're reviewing our processes to ensure every stakeholder feels heard and valued as we move forward together."

Which phrase does the most work while saying the least, and who does it protect?

What the language is doing
Every phrase manages your feelings while promising nothing. "Mistakes were made." Passive, agentless. Mistakes appear from nowhere; no person is behind them. "Doing the work" / "reviewing our processes." Motion words with no action attached. Reviewing is not changing. "Feels heard and valued." A feeling, not a fact. They promise your emotion, not their behavior. That gap between what a text performs and what it actually commits to is exactly what a discourse analysis exposes.
