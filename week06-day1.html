Start where we left off
"Our city is committed to helping individuals experiencing homelessness connect with the services they need while ensuring that parks and public spaces remain safe and accessible for everyone." A sentence of the kind you'd find on any municipal webpage.

Last week we'd have called this rhetorical analysis. What do you notice?

Some of what's in there
The languageWhat it's doing
"committed"Establishes institutional ethos. The city is a good actor before any claim is made.
"individuals experiencing homelessness"Person-first language. A deliberate choice, and a recent one.
"services they need"Implies a particular understanding of the problem: a need for services.
"safe and accessible"Positions homelessness in tension with public safety.
"everyone"Raises the question of who is imagined as the public, and who isn't.
The problem

All of that is useful. Now imagine I have forty of these pages instead of one sentence.

I need some systematic way to keep track of what I'm seeing across all of them. That's coding.

Two different jobs
Rhetorical analysisCoding
Closely examines how a text creates meaningSystematically labels features across a set of texts
Asks, "what is this language doing?"Asks, "what should I call this thing I keep seeing?"
Often produces interpretationProduces organized data that can later be interpreted
May closely examine a few examplesHelps researchers compare many examples
"Safe and accessible" constructs homelessness partly as a public-space problemPublic safety frame
Coding does not replace rhetorical analysis. It gives you a systematic way of doing it across a corpus. Same attention to language. Applied at a scale where you'd otherwise lose track of your own observations.
Definition

A code is a short label researchers use to identify something meaningful that appears in their data.

A code has to be specific enough that you know when to use it, and specific enough that someone unfamiliar with your codebook could pick it up and apply it to texts you've never shown them.

The rule that comes out of that
If you cannot explain what textual evidence would cause you to apply the code, the code isn't ready yet.

Workshop · build three codes
Your research question for this exercise: How do local institutions explain the causes of homelessness, and the appropriate responses to it?
A"Substance abuse and untreated mental illness remain major contributors to chronic homelessness."
B"The county has expanded shelter capacity and behavioral-health outreach to help residents return to stable housing."
C"Rents have increased nearly 30 percent while wages for low-income workers have remained largely stagnant."
D"Residents have raised concerns about drug use, trash, and threatening behavior near downtown businesses."

On your own, write three codes that would help a researcher analyze patterns across these four. Each one needs a code, a definition, an include-when, and an example.

Do not try to code everything. Your research question decides what matters. Anything that doesn't help you answer it can be left on the page.

One more column
This is why researchers add exclusion criteria. The hard part of coding is rarely what obviously belongs. It's what happens at the edges. Take one of your three codes and add an Exclude when… rule to it now.
