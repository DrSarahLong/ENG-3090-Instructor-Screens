Can a graph be technically accurate and still be misleading?

You are about to turn other people's answers into pictures. That is a kind of power, and it is the last place in this course where you can still be unfair to your participants without meaning to be.

Neither chart contains a false number. Which one would you rather have published about your research?

Here it is

Chart A's vertical axis starts at 50%. Chart B's starts at zero.

A four-point difference becomes a cliff, and every number on the chart is correct. The length of a bar is what represents the quantity. Cut the bottom off the axis and the lengths stop meaning what the eye assumes they mean. A technically correct number can be visually dishonest.
Four rules

Ethics did not end when you finished collecting data

Do not generalize beyond your sample. “Of the 32 respondents in this study…” and not “Americans believe…” Your data can only speak for the people you actually reached.
Show the N you actually have. With samples this size, “18 of 27 respondents (67%)” tells the truth that “67%” alone hides. Your reader deserves to know how many human beings are behind the percentage.
Do not visually exaggerate differences. Bar charts start at zero. No truncated axes, no inconsistent intervals, no 3-D.
Do not cherry-pick. Report the inconvenient results too, and do not pull the one dramatic open-ended response and let it stand for everybody.

Discuss

So what happened to the number?

Jordan did not change a single figure. What did he change?

Here it is

He changed its scale, its form, and whether a human being could comprehend it.

Sometimes the problem with data is not that we lack it. The problem is that people are terrible at imagining scale. “Two million” is meaningful and almost nobody can picture two million objects. So visualization performs a translation: it turns data into something a human perceptual system can take in. That is rhetorical power, and power carries responsibility. Your job is not to make the data dramatic. Your job is to make the data visible. Sometimes those are the same thing and sometimes they are not.

The short answer

Which graph do I use?

You want to show…Use…
Differences between categoriesBar chart
Responses across a scale, strongly disagree to strongly agreeBar chart or stacked bar
Change over timeLine graph
Exact values, or a small amount of informationTable
Themes, quotations, qualitative findingsText or a thematic table

Almost all of you will use bar charts, and that is fine. There are no methodological sophistication points for finding a stranger graph.

One anti-rule

Avoid pie charts unless you have a simple part-to-whole relationship with only a few categories.

A bar chart lets the eye compare values faster, which is usually the thing you wanted. And no 3-D pie charts.

Put these together

What does the quotation add that the number does not?

The number21 of 30 respondents identified housing affordability as a major contributor to homelessness.
An open-ended responsePeople keep saying just get a job, but the jobs around here don't pay enough to cover rent.
Here it is

The number tells you how common it was. The quotation tells you how respondents understood it.

Number→ Pattern→ Qualitative evidence→ Interpretation
Written out: Twenty-one of 30 respondents identified housing affordability as an important contributor to homelessness. Open-ended responses frequently connected housing costs to local wages, with one respondent writing, “…” Together, these findings suggest that respondents understood affordability not simply as high rent, but as a mismatch between housing costs and income. That is mixed-methods integration. Four moves, one paragraph.
For the focus-group project

You do not need a graph.

Do not write66.7% of participants believed housing was the biggest problem.
WriteFour of the six participants identified housing costs as a major concern.

Or, if the frequency is not the point: Housing affordability emerged as one of the strongest themes in the discussion. Then show the evidence. With a group this small, turning everything into percentages makes the report weaker, not more rigorous.

For the focus-group project

Organize Results around themes, and show the interaction.

Housing affordability. Explain the pattern, quote a participant, then show whether somebody agreed, complicated it, or disagreed.
Stigma and personal responsibility. Explain, quote, show the interaction.
Availability of local resources. Explain, quote, show the disagreement or the consensus.

The exchange itself is a finding. One participant emphasizes individual choices, another answers with wages and rent, and the conversation moves. That cannot go in a pie chart, and it should not.
