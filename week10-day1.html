The move

Our running topic is homelessness. Our broad question: how do people in our region understand the causes of and possible responses to homelessness? Two ways to ask it.

Question AWhy do you think so many homeless people refuse to work?
Question BWhat factors do you think contribute to homelessness in our region?
Question A has already named a cause, that people do not work, and already decided that not working is a choice. Whatever comes back is partly our own sentence returned to us. Question B lets the participant tell us what they think. Everything else today is one skill: do not put your answer inside your question.
Before we write anything

Last week's three principles, turned into three questions you can use.

The Belmont Report, 1979, written by a federal commission after the Tuskegee syphilis study became public. It is the document American research ethics still runs on.

Respect

Are people choosing freely to participate, and do they understand what we are asking them to do?

Beneficence

Could our questions embarrass, stigmatize, distress, identify, or otherwise harm someone?

Justice

Why are we asking these people to supply the data?

That third one lands differently on this topic. If we were interviewing people experiencing homelessness, we would have to think about power, vulnerability, compensation, privacy, trauma, and whether somebody might believe that access to services depended on talking to us.
And one more principle

If you do not need a piece of information to answer your research question, do not collect it.

A question worth asking is not the same as a question you are entitled to ask.
Worth asking, and we mayWhat do you think contributes to homelessness in this region?

It answers our research question, and nobody is exposed by answering it.
Worth asking, and we may notHave you ever been arrested?

A researcher might find that useful. Our question is about how people understand causes, so an arrest record tells us nothing we need and could put a participant at risk.
Three methods

These are not three interchangeable ways of asking questions.

MethodAnswers the questionEspecially good for
SURVEYHow much? How many? How often? How strongly?Patterns, frequencies, attitudes across more people
INTERVIEWHow does someone understand, experience, or explain this?Individual experience, explanation, meaning
FOCUS GROUPHow do people work this out in front of each other?Shared belief, disagreement, the words a community uses
A survey might tell you that 62% of respondents believe a shortage of affordable housing is a major cause of homelessness. It will not tell you why they believe it or what they mean by affordable. The question is never which method is easiest. It is what kind of evidence my research question requires.
Pick one, or combine them

You can run a survey, interviews, a focus group, or a combination.

Most of you will land on a survey with some open-ended questions, and that is a sound choice. It gets you counts from a decent number of people and quotes you can use in your write-up. It also means you need both skill sets today. Your closed questions follow the survey rules we are about to go through. Your open-ended questions follow the interview rules in the second half, because an open-ended survey question is an interview question that nobody is there to follow up on. Which makes the wording matter more, not less. You get one shot at it and no chance to say “can you tell me more about that?”
Surveys

Survey questions look easy. They are not.

One clear, neutral question that your respondent can answer. Let's see how fast we can break that.
Problem 1 · Leading
BrokenDon't you agree that Boone should provide more shelter beds for people experiencing homelessness?
It tells the respondent which answer we expect.
BetterDo you think the number of emergency shelter beds in Boone should increase, decrease, or stay about the same?
Another versionWhat, if anything, do you think local government should do in response to homelessness?
Those two words, if anything, make “nothing” a legitimate answer.
Problem 2 · Loaded
BrokenHow concerned are you about the growing homelessness crisis downtown?
Three assumptions ride along: that it is growing, that it is a crisis, and that you ought to be concerned. All three might be correct. A survey question still cannot help itself to them.
BetterHow concerned, if at all, are you about homelessness in downtown Boone?
Problem 3 · Double-barreled
BrokenDo you think local governments should provide housing and mental-health services for people experiencing homelessness?
That is two policies in one question. Somebody who supports housing and opposes the proposed program has no way to say so, so their yes means nothing reliable.
BetterDo you think local governments should provide additional housing assistance for people experiencing homelessness?
Another versionThen ask about mental-health services as a separate question.
One question, one idea.
Problem 4 · Asking what they cannot know
BrokenHow many people experiencing homelessness in Boone have a mental illness?
Nobody filling out your survey knows that number. They have an impression, and an impression is a different thing. If perception is what you want, ask for perception.
BetterIn your opinion, how important a factor is untreated mental illness in contributing to homelessness?
Problem 5 · Absolutes
BrokenDo you agree that everyone experiencing homelessness should be offered permanent housing?
Be suspicious of always, never, all, none, everyone. Sometimes the concept you are studying is that absolute and the word belongs. More often an absolute forces people into a position they do not hold, and then you report a disagreement you manufactured.
BetterShould permanent housing be offered to all people experiencing homelessness, to some, or to none?
Problem 6 · Undefined terms
BrokenDo you think homelessness is a serious problem in our community?
Community could mean campus, Boone, the High Country, or Tennessee. Homelessness could mean sleeping outside, a shelter, a motel paid week to week, or a friend's couch. Deciding what your concepts mean is called operationalization, and you have to do it before you can ask about them.
BetterFor the purposes of this survey, experiencing homelessness includes people staying in shelters, living outdoors or in vehicles, or otherwise lacking a fixed nighttime residence. Do you think homelessness is a serious problem in Boone?
Problem 7 · Answer choices that don't fit
BrokenHow often do you encounter people experiencing homelessness?   Very often / Often / Sometimes / Rarely
There is no Never, and often no I'm not sure. A respondent who has never encountered anyone has to pick Rarely, which is a lie you built for them.
BetterHow often do you encounter people experiencing homelessness?   Very often / Often / Sometimes / Rarely / Never / I'm not sure
Problem 8 · Overlapping categories
BrokenWhat is your age?   18–25 / 25–35 / 35–45 / 45+
A 25-year-old belongs in two boxes. It looks like a small detail and it ruins the data, because you cannot tell which respondents landed where.
BetterWhat is your age?   18–24 / 25–34 / 35–44 / 45+
Problem 9 · Only one acceptable answer
BrokenDo you believe people experiencing homelessness deserve to be treated with dignity?
Almost everyone says yes, so all we measured is whether people know the acceptable answer. You get better data by asking about a choice or a trade-off, because a trade-off makes people spend something.
BetterWhich of the following local responses to homelessness would you be most likely to support?   Emergency shelter / Permanent supportive housing / Rental assistance / Substance-use treatment / Mental-health services / Increased policing / Another approach / None of these
Order matters

Opening a survey with Have you personally experienced homelessness? is an awfully intimate way to say hello.

Easy → specific → sensitive. Start broad, get narrower, and put sensitive personal and demographic questions late unless you have a methodological reason to do otherwise. And give people Prefer not to answer. A respondent who has to lie or quit is a respondent you lost either way.
Interviews, and open-ended survey questions

Here the logic flips.

A closed survey question wants a controlled answer. An open question wants the person to talk. So the yes-or-no question, which was fine a moment ago, doesn't work.
For your project, go semi-structured. Prepare core questions so everybody gets roughly the same major ones, then let yourself follow an answer that goes somewhere. Enough consistency to compare participants, without turning the interview into a survey read out loud.
The same question, three ways
ClosedDo you think affordable housing contributes to homelessness?
BetterWhat role, if any, do you think housing costs play in homelessness in this region?
Better stillWhen you think about homelessness in this region, what causes or contributing factors come to mind?
The third is better because it doesn't lead or suggest. The first hands them a yes-or-no. The second puts housing costs in their head before they answer. The third asks what comes to mind and then you follow where they go.
Write this one down

“Can you tell me more about that?”

That one will rescue you repeatedly. These four do the same job, and here is why none of them hands over an answer.

What do you mean by that?Asks them to define their own word. You have not offered a definition for them to agree with.
Can you give me an example?Asks for their evidence. You have not suggested what would count as one.
What happened next?Asks for sequence. There is no claim in it at all.
Why do you think that?Asks for their reasoning. Compare it to “Is that because of housing costs?”, which hands them the reason.
Do not interview yourself
Students do this constantlyDo you think homelessness has gotten worse because housing has become so expensive?
That is not an interview question. That is your opinion or argument with a question mark at the end.
Ask insteadHave you noticed any changes in homelessness in this area?
ThenWhat do you think might explain those changes?
An odd skill

Stop talking.

They answer. They pause. Your instinct is to say “Great, next question.” Don't. Wait. People fill silence, and the second thing they say is frequently more interesting and in-depth than the first.
Focus groups
A question built for a groupWhen you hear people in this community talk about homelessness, what kinds of explanations do you hear?
It asks about the community's talk rather than the participant's own position, so nobody has to defend a belief. People report what they have heard, and then they start correcting each other. Mostly addiction. — I hear housing costs much more often. — Depends who you're talking to. That exchange is the data. You are facilitating a conversation among participants, not conducting five interviews at the same time.
Two dangers

One person talks for twenty minutes and everybody else nods. That is an interview with witnesses.

Your job as moderator is to distribute participation, and you can do it without embarrassing anybody. “We've heard a couple of perspectives. I'd like to hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet.”
Does anyone see this differently?Is that how others understand it, or are there competing explanations?
You are not manufacturing disagreement. You are making room for it.
The second is the more serious one. In an interview you can protect what somebody tells you. In a focus group everyone hears everything. You can ask participants not to repeat it, but you cannot guarantee they won't, so some questions do not belong in a group at all. Tell the group about the circumstances that led you to become homeless. Which puts us back where the hour started. A question that would produce striking data is not therefore a question you should ask.
