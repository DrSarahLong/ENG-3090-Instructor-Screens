Finding & Vetting Sources

Find out what's already known, then decide who to trust.
Debate this

"Real evidence comes from published studies and official data. One person's lived experience is just an anecdote."

You've got a question. Who already tried to answer something like it?

Where would you even start looking?

Why we cite: to leave breadcrumbs. Every reference list at the end of a paper is a trail. Follow other people's. Leave a clear one of your own. The reference list is also a shortcut: mine a good source's bibliography and it hands you the next five.
Where to actually look

Belk Library databases

Full text of peer-reviewed work you can't reach from the open web.

Google Scholar

Searches scholarship, not the open web. Free, and it shows who cited whom.

AI tools that show sources

Perplexity searches the live web and cites its links. Elicit finds and summarizes scientific papers for a research question. Consensus searches peer-reviewed studies and tells you what the research actually concludes.

Mine the bibliography

Open a good source, read its reference list, chase the ones that fit.

The caveat: AI invents sources that look real. Never cite anything you haven't opened and read.
How to vet what you find · SIFT

Stop

Before you share or cite, pause. Do you even know what this is? e.g., the top hit for "are eco-labels honest" is a glossy page from a labeling trade group, don't trust the ranking.

Investigate the source

Who made this, and why? Leave the page to find out. e.g., the "Center for Food Integrity" turns out to be an industry PR group, look them up before you read a word.

Find better coverage

Look for a stronger source on the same claim. e.g., instead of one blog saying "natural means nothing," pull a peer-reviewed study on how shoppers read the word.

Trace the claim

Follow it back to where it started. e.g., a headline says "73% of shoppers are fooled", you click through and the actual study says 31%.

Whose sources count? · Trust it, depends, or skip

Which of these do you trust, and why?

Don't ask "does it look legit?" Ask "who made this, and why?" Not one of these is as simple as it looks, run each through SIFT.

1

A peer-reviewed study concluding "natural" labels don't mislead, funded by an egg-producers' association.

2

A polished .org site, the "Center for Food Integrity," that turns out to be an industry PR group.

3

A plain, ad-cluttered blog that cites six real studies and is written by a food scientist.

4

An FDA page defining "healthy" on packaging, last updated in 2016.

5

A news headline, "73% of shoppers fooled by green labels", but the study it links says 31%.

6

A Perplexity answer with five citations, two of which vanish when you click them.
