The Workshop

A working session, not a lecture.

Review · the nine

Run every question you write against this list.

1 · Leading

It tells the respondent which answer you expect.

“Don't you agree Boone should provide more shelter beds?”

2 · Loaded

It smuggles in assumptions the respondent never agreed to.

“How concerned are you about the growing homelessness crisis?”

3 · Double-barreled

Two ideas in one question, so a single answer cannot cover both.

“Should government provide housing and mental-health services?”

4 · Asking what they cannot know

It requests a fact the respondent has no access to, so you collect a guess.

“How many people experiencing homelessness have a mental illness?”

5 · Absolutes

Always, never, all, everyone force people into positions they do not hold.

“Should everyone be offered permanent housing?”

6 · Undefined terms

Key words mean different things to different respondents, so answers are not comparable.

“Is homelessness a serious problem in our community?”

7 · Answer choices that don't fit

No option describes the respondent's actual situation, so they pick a near-lie.

Very often / Often / Sometimes / Rarely, with no Never.

8 · Overlapping categories

A respondent belongs in two boxes and you cannot tell which they chose.

18–25 / 25–35 / 35–45 / 45+

9 · Only one acceptable answer

Everyone gives the same reply, so you measured etiquette rather than opinion.

“Do people experiencing homelessness deserve dignity?”

Two words that fix a surprising number of these: if anything, and if at all. They make “nothing” and “not at all” legitimate answers.

The work · in this order

What you are doing for the rest of class.

Write your consent statement first. Use the model consent language from the App State IRB office, adapted to your study. This is not a formality you dash off: it is the human-subjects ethics we covered in Week 9, and it is the first thing a participant reads.It needs to cover who you are and that this is coursework, what the study is about, how long it takes, that participation is voluntary and can stop at any time, what happens to the responses, and how to reach you.
Draft your questions, and balance the two kinds. Closed-ended questions (multiple choice, scales, yes/no) are what you count, chart, and compare, so most of your instrument has to be closed or you will have nothing to visualize later. Open-ended questions are where people say something you did not anticipate, and they give you material to quote. Too few open questions and your report is all numbers with no voices in it. Too many and you have a pile of text you cannot analyze in the time you have.A workable shape for a ten-minute intercept survey: eight to ten closed questions, two open ones at the end. Order them so you ease into anything tough or sensitive. Start with the easy, factual, low-stakes items; put the questions people might hesitate over after they have already answered a few things comfortably.
Run every question against the nine.

The focus group · building it

You are writing a facilitator guide, not a questionnaire.

Five or six open questions in a deliberate sequence, each with two or three follow-up probes underneath it. Budget the time before you write the questions. A 60-minute session with six questions is ten minutes each, and in practice the first question runs long and the last one gets three minutes. So order them by what you most need: the question you cannot leave without an answer to goes third or fourth, while people are warm and the clock is not yet against you. Open with something easy that gets every voice into the room once, because a person who has not spoken in the first ten minutes often does not speak at all.
The focus group · consent

Your consent form is different from everyone else's in this room.

The survey consent statements being written around you promise anonymity. Yours cannot. Your form has to say that other participants will hear every answer, so responses are confidential from the outside but not private within the group. It needs ground rules participants agree to, including that what is said in the room stays there, which you ask for even though you cannot enforce it. And permission to record is a separate question, asked before you start recording, not a line buried in the paragraph they skimmed.
The focus group · running it

Managing the room is most of the method.

Recording

Two devices, because one will fail. Test the audio where people will actually sit, not next to you. State the date and the group aloud at the start so the file identifies itself. Watch your battery.

Dynamics to manage

One person dominating; the group converging on whoever spoke first; deference to the person perceived as most senior; a quiet participant holding the view nobody else will say.

Moves that work

“I'd like to hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet.” “Does anyone see it differently?” And silence: count to five before you fill a pause. The answer that arrives after an uncomfortable gap is usually the one you came for.

Nonverbals to log

Who looks at whom before answering. Who goes quiet right after a particular person speaks. Nodding or head-shaking that never becomes speech. Someone leaning back and checking out. Note these as they happen, because the recording will not catch them.

Listen to the recording twice. Once for what was said, and once for how: who hedged, who got interrupted, where the room went quiet, where several people started talking at once. The second pass is where a focus group earns its cost over a survey.
Before you pack up

Sketch where, when, and who.

A first pass, not a final plan: which building, which days, what times, and roughly how many responses you are aiming for. You will firm this up Thursday when we do recruitment properly, and it is much easier to revise a plan than to invent one.

While you sketch, think about what time of day gets you a crowd that is not your own friend group. The 8 a.m. crowd and the 9 p.m. crowd are not the same people.
