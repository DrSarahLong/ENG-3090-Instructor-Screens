The steps
Find the gap, and name the injustice. What has the literature not answered, and what is at stake in it? If your study never happened, what would the world lose? That answer is your "so what," and it comes out of your literature review.

Two different machines, two different jobs

Get this straight before you open anything.

Voyant counts wordsIt ranks them, shows every place a word appears in context, finds which words cluster together, and measures how long and how varied your documents are. It cannot judge a frame. "Safe" is a string of four letters to it. It has no idea who is being kept safe from whom.
AI reads for meaningHand it your codebook and a text and it can apply your codes as a first pass. That is what it is built to do, and it will do in ninety seconds what would take you an hour. It will also be confidently wrong some of the time, which is why you verify it.
So the division of labor is: Voyant tells you where to look and describes what you gathered. AI does the first coding pass. You verify, resolve the hard cases, and decide what it means. The judgment is the part that is yours.

First look: the word cloud

It is a bad finding and a good codebook tool.

Never use it as evidenceIt shows frequency and nothing else. Size means "appears often," not "matters." It strips every word out of the context your analysis depends on. "Safe" appearing large tells you nothing about who is being kept safe from whom. Do not put one in P2.
Use it to build your codesIt shows you the vocabulary of the conversation before you've decided what to look for. Words you expected turn out to be rare. Words you never considered are everywhere. That is codebook design: the cloud tells you which words are worth building a code around, and which of your codes have nothing to grab onto in this corpus.
Look at the cloud for our three texts. Which words would you build a code around, and which of last week's codes look like they'll find nothing here? That's the whole use of it. Then close the tab.

If "patrols" appears 9 times and 8 of them are in Text 1, you cannot write "this coverage emphasizes enforcement." None of those numbers is a finding. Vocabulary density of 0.412 is not a result. It is a description of the pile of paper you gathered.
Every measure on that table belongs in the corpus paragraph of your Methods. Findings are about your codes. Methods are about your corpus. Students lose this criterion every year by reporting the tool's dashboard as their analysis.
Write it now, in your notebook

Methods, paragraph one: your corpus.

This analysis draws on a corpus of [N] texts totaling [N] words, gathered from [what kinds of sources] published between [dates]. Documents range from [shortest] to [longest] words. Texts were selected because [your inclusion rule]; I excluded [what, and why]. The corpus is small enough that I read every text closely, and I make no claim that it represents [the larger population].
Overstating is the fastest way to lose a reader you already had. Claim three texts' worth and a reader trusts your three texts. Claim that three texts reveal how America talks about homelessness and they stop trusting anything else in the paper, including the parts you got right.

Now run "safe" in the concordance

Three hits. Three incompatible meanings.

"Our visitors do not feel safe, and they are telling us so." Text 1 · safe FROM homeless people
"But housing is what makes a person safe. A door that locks is what makes a person safe." Text 2 · safe MEANS housed
"Every person we place is a person who has a safe place to sleep that night." Text 3 · safe FOR a homeless person, for one night
One frames homelessness as a danger to other people. One frames housing itself as safety. One means a bed, tonight. A word count cannot tell them apart. A reader can. This is why the footprint is a search strategy and not an answer. Voyant just narrowed three texts down to the three passages worth arguing about. Arguing about them is the analysis.
Do this now

Code the corpus, passage by passage.

Make a table: one row per coded passage. Columns for text, the quoted passage, the code, and why it qualifies under your include rule.
Work through your concordance hits. That is your list of candidate passages.
When a passage is ambiguous, do not force it. Log it as contested with one line about why. Text 3 is a good example: it uses service vocabulary to argue that services are being misused. Is that your service code or not? Defend your answer.
If you change a code while coding, go back and re-check the texts you already did. Otherwise your first text was coded under different rules than your last.
Using AI for this, ethically and correctly

It is a first-pass coder. You are the coder of record.

Paste your full codebook, definitions and include/exclude rules and all, then one text. Ask it to identify every passage matching each code and to quote them exactly.
Ask it to explain why each passage qualifies. If the reason doesn't match your include rule, either your rule is vague or the model is guessing. Both are worth knowing, and fixing the rule is the actual work.
Verify. For every text, check that at least two of its quoted passages actually appear in the text, word for word, and do fit the code.
Write what you did in Methods: which model, what you asked it, and how you checked. Unreported AI coding is a hole in your method that any reader can put a finger through.
Two failure modes. It will produce quotations that sound right and are not in your text. And it will agree with whatever frame your codebook implies, because it is built to be agreeable. Neither is a reason to avoid it. Both are reasons to check it.

Write it now

One finding, four moves.

State the pattern. What you counted, across how much. Numbers, plainly.
Show it. Quote one passage that typifies the code. One strong quote beats three weak ones.
Complicate it. Name the text that doesn't fit, or the passage you marked contested.
Say what it means, for power and for justice. Who does this framing serve? Who is present in it, who is only spoken about, and who is not in it at all? What would change if the other frame won?
Across the [N] texts, [CODE] appeared in [N] passages in [N] of [N] documents, at [N] per thousand words, concentrated in [which genres]. A representative instance: "[quote]" ([text]). The pattern is not uniform: [the text that resists it] instead [what it does]. Read together, these texts [the claim about power]. The people described in them [who speaks, who is spoken about], and that matters because [what is at stake, i.e. your "so what"].
What that looks like filled in
Across the three texts, the word "safe" appears in all three and means something different in each. In the merchants' account it means safe from homeless people; in the opinion column it means housed; in the nonprofit newsletter it means a bed for one night. Only the merchants' usage is attached to a proposed action, and that action is enforcement: patrols, ordinances, a safety officer. The column and the newsletter both attach safety to housing and shelter, and neither proposes anything a city could do by Tuesday. The pattern is not uniform. The newsletter uses service vocabulary to argue that services are being misused as "a softer form of removal," which is the only place in the corpus where the people being discussed are described as understanding what is happening to them. Across all three texts, no unhoused person speaks. They are counted, quoted about, and advocated for, in that order. That matters because the frame with the clearest action attached to it is also the one built entirely from the point of view of people who are not homeless. Numbers, a quote, a complication, and a claim about power and justice. ~180 words.
